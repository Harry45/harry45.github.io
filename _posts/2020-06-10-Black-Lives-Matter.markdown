<p align="justify">Since the death of George Floyd on 25 May 2020 (see the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_of_George_Floyd">Wikipedia</a> article), protests have continued not only in the US but around the world, as people voice their concerns about systemic racism and the injustices faced by Black people. Having grown up in Mauritius, a multicultural, multilingual and multi-ethnic society, I feel a deep connection with this issue, and in this post I would like to share some of my views.</p>

<p align="justify">Our past shapes our future. In Mauritius, although our ancestors were subjected to slavery (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mauritius">History of Mauritius</a>), today we enjoy full freedom of religion, of the press, of speech and much more, which places us among the highest-ranked countries for these freedoms. That said, I am not claiming that my island is the paradise it is often portrayed as internationally. We still have much work to do to combat poverty, crime and corruption, among other problems, and I am almost certain that every country faces similar issues.</p>

<p align="justify">My journey then took me to Cape Town, where I spent two years completing my MSc. South Africa lived through apartheid, and I could still feel the tension among people. While I was there, students at the University of Cape Town were protesting for the removal of the Rhodes memorial (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes_Must_Fall">Rhodes Must Fall</a>). On Facebook, Black people were repeatedly referred to as 'Black Monkeys', which affected me deeply. Are we living in a society where people pretend everything is fine? I remember a friend telling me, 'you don't know what those people did to our parents and grandparents'. Another friend told me that his grandfather had to live with a bullet in his face, because doctors said that removing it would kill him.</p>

<p align="justify">It strikes me that we often respond according to the 'action-reaction' principle (Newton's third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction). Without the protests over the killing of Black people, there might have been no (re)action at all. My question is: why do we need to label people as Black, White or Coloured at all? Why must we be categorised by the colour of our skin? Coming from Mauritius, I do not even know how to describe my ethnic background, yet I am often asked to state it on forms. On a positive note, I am glad that various initiatives have been introduced since the protests; two that I am aware of are:</p>
<ol>
  <li>The Presidential Scholarship for Black Students (<a href="https://www.imperial.ac.uk/giving/donate/black-scholarship/"><i style="font-size:12px" class="fa">&#xf08e;</i></a>)</li>
  <li>Google commitments to racial equity (<a href="https://www.blog.google/inside-google/company-announcements/commitments-racial-equity/"><i style="font-size:12px" class="fa">&#xf08e;</i></a>)</li>
</ol>

<p align="justify">I believe we can do more to address (systemic) racism in our society. I am certain that many people in Africa do not know how to take advantage of these opportunities. They need to be made aware of the process and made to feel that they belong. I am grateful to my colleagues that we now have sessions dedicated to BAME (Black, Asian and Minority Ethnic) issues. I strongly believe that education is the main pillar of a fair and equal society. If one department at each of, say, 100 universities in Europe and the US funded at least one student from Africa every year for ten years, at least 1,000 students would gain access to a world-class education. I remember a saying from my parents, 'be a light that can never be extinguished', and I firmly believe that education, above all, is what enables a person to make something of their life.</p>
